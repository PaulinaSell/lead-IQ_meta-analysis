Introduction

The aim of this project is to estimate the Environmental Burden of Disease (EBD) attributable to lead exposure in Germany. Here, we will only focus on the neurodevelopmental effects of lead, therefore the health outcome will be IQ point loss in children and adolescents in Germany. In order to estimate these population health effects, we need to find solid evidence for exactly how lead exposure is related to IQ loss. The exposure data we are working with is based on blood tests of ~720 children and adolescents (ages 3 - 17) in Germany from the German Environmental Survey, GerES V. The data collection period was from 2014 to 2017.
In order to quantify exactly how lead exposure is related to IQ loss, we consult the epidemiological literature. There are many studies published focusing on the the neurodevelopmental effects of lead. Instead of just choosing one estimate for our EBD analysis, we could try to pool estimates from multiple studies in order to strengthen the evidence we base our analysis on. And since uncertainty plays an important role in the final burden of disease results, we will employ a Bayesian approach. With this approach we naturally model uncertainty and get a full posterior distribution of the pooled effect (instead of a mean effect and an hard-to-interpret confidence interval). Later, we will be able to use many draws from the posterior distribution for our EBD analysis! But first, let’s meta analyse some epi studies.

Step 1: Standardizing Effect Estimates

We first need to search for and identify relevant studies and extract relevant data. These steps were already performed by Philippe Palmont and his team at ANSES.
Now, we still need to harmonize / standardize the effect estimates before we can pool them. Most of the studies identified transformed their blood lead level (BLL) data to a logarithmic scale in order to fulfill the assumption of a linear relationship between BLL (exposure) and IQ points (outcome). Unfortunately, they did not always use the same bases. Some used log10, some ln, and some log2. Also, 3 of the 14 included studies did not transform their BLL at all, so it’s on a linear scale. Philippe Palmont and his team standardized all estimates to linear increments of exposure in their analysis, using the template proposed by Rodriguez Barranco 2017. But what happens to the effect estimates, when we transform them in this way?

Visually Checking Transformations

Let’s compare the original beta coefficient from Crump et al. 2013 (ß = -2.65) for ln(BLL) with the transformed one (ß = ß -0.277), that now should be used for linear BLL.

This looks like we would likely underestimate the effect of lower BLL and overestimate the effect of higher BLL. Since today, BLL are found to be much lower than even 30 µg/dL (in Germany, the range is somewhere from 0.04 to 4.5 µg/dL in children and adolescents), we would likely underestimate the population health effects of BLL on IQ in children. Transforming log estimates to linear seems to be problematic here.

Transforming Estimates to ln(BLL)

In order to preserve the correct exposure-response curves and harmonize the effect estimates for our meta analysis, we could try to transform all estimates to ln(BLL), since this closer to the original estimates reported by the studies, since they mostly just used different bases for their logarithmic transformations.

These are the formulas I used:
βln = βlinear × Cref
βln = βlog2 ÷ ln (2)
βln = βlog10 ÷ ln (10)

Where:
βln = harmonized beta coefficient (natural logarithm)
βlinear = original linear beta coefficient
βlog2 = original log₂-transformed beta coefficient
βlog10 = original log₁₀-transformed beta coefficient
Cref = reference concentration for linear transformation
